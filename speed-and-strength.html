Every session makes a player better or worse. There's no middle.

Off-ice training fails two ways, and both are common.

Too soft

The session is a workout, not a stimulus. The player sweats, gets tired, goes home sore, and nothing measurable changes. A year of that and the numbers look exactly like they did last September.

Wrong kind

Worse than doing nothing. Loading a young athlete badly builds strength in the wrong places, hides the weak links instead of fixing them, and quietly sets up the back, hips and shoulders to fail two seasons later.

Are you paying for your kid to be sore and tired, or for training that shows up on the ice?

Built by someone the wrong training broke.

I did everything I was told at a hockey-specific gym. I trusted it. It left me with chronic back problems and shoulders that needed multiple surgeries, and it cost me the shot I'd worked my whole life for.

Nobody was checking whether the work was making me better. It was making me tired, and we all assumed that was the same thing.

That's why our gym is built the way it is. Every player who trains here should leave more durable than they arrived — not just stronger.

— Luke, founder

You can't train what you haven't measured.

Most gyms run a program and hope. We test first — speed over real distances on laser timers, jump and force numbers, single-leg strength, and the mobility that decides whether a player can even get into a powerful position.

Those numbers do three things. They put a player in the right training group instead of a generic one. They show us the weak link that's actually holding them back. And they give you something better than "I think I see a difference" — the same tests, run again, with the results side by side.

Three ideas that change how a player trains.

Size isn't strength.

A gymnast who's never touched a barbell can hold his whole bodyweight on two rings. He isn't big. His nervous system is just exceptional at using what he has. Strength is a skill before it's a size — and a player who trains the skill gets far stronger without adding weight he has to drag up the ice.

Faster first, bigger second.

The best players in the world aren't the biggest. They're among the fastest. Speed survives every level a player moves up. Size is the thing kids chase in the wrong window and pay for in the first stride. We never trade one for the other.

The invisible governor.

A player can squat a house and still be slow. If an ankle can't handle the load, the body refuses to send force through it — so everything above it is capped. Fix the weak link and strength the player already had shows up the same week.

How we train

Triphasic, built for hockey.

Our programming is built on Cal Dietz's triphasic system — training the eccentric, isometric and concentric phases of a movement as separate qualities rather than treating a rep as one thing. It maps unusually well onto skating, where a player is absorbing force on one leg and redirecting it constantly.

What it means in practice: blocks with a purpose, in an order that builds on itself, progressed off the numbers we tested rather than a calendar.

The three things that end hockey seasons.

Groins and hips. Lower backs. Shoulders. They're not bad luck — they're the predictable result of a sport that loads one side, one plane, and one position for eight months a year.

We train against that from day one. Not as a warm-up bolted onto the front of a session, but as part of the programming itself. A player who misses six weeks in February loses more than a player who trained slightly less hard and stayed on the ice.

Strength is what the skills run on.

Every skill on the ice is limited by what the body underneath it can do. A player who physically can't get low can't hold an edge, no matter how well the technique is coached. A shot is force transferred through the ground. Getting faster stops being a skating problem once technique is sound and becomes a strength problem.
